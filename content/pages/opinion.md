## hero

eyebrow: Module 3 · Public Opinion in Arizona
title: How Arizona feels about politics — and each other

The Arizona Voter Project, in collaboration with the Arizona Policy Lab at the University of Arizona, regularly conducts survey-based research. This module reports results from the <em>Arizona Voter Survey</em> — a scientific survey of likely voters, with a sample constructed to mirror the population that participates in Arizona elections. Read across seven themes: affective polarization, the partisan attitude profile, political tolerance, democratic norms, issue positions, feeling thermometers, and the gap between what partisans <em>think</em> the other side feels and what it actually feels. Group aggregates only — no individual responses.

## scales

eyebrow: Reading these numbers

Many measures are rescaled to run <b>0–100</b> so questions with different answer choices sit on one ruler: <b>0</b> = the lowest answer the survey allowed, <b>100</b> = the highest, <b>50</b> = the scale&rsquo;s midpoint. These are <em>not percentages of people</em> — &ldquo;abortion-restrictive 58&rdquo; means the average Republican sits toward the restrictive end of the scale, not that 58% hold a view. Thermometers are already a natural 0–100 (cold→warm); the 1–5 scales are shown on their own 1–5 range.

## affect

eyebrow: Theme 1 · Affective polarization
title: How warmly do partisans feel — toward their own side, and the other?

From feeling thermometers (0–100). <b>In-party warmth</b> is how warm a respondent feels toward their own party; <b>out-party negativity</b> is 100 minus their warmth toward the other; <b>affective polarization</b> is the gap between the two.

## norms

eyebrow: Theme 3 · Democratic norms &amp; tolerance
title: Commitment to democratic norms, and tolerance of the disliked

Both are 1–5 scales, coded so <b>higher = more democratic / more tolerant</b>. <b>Democratic norms</b> is support for democratic procedure over its violation. <b>Tolerance</b> is content-controlled: each respondent named the group they like <em>least</em>, and was then asked whether <em>that</em> group should be allowed to speak, teach, hold office, and so on — the classic Sullivan–Marcus measure. Notably, tolerance is roughly equal across parties; commitment to norms is not.

## issues

eyebrow: Theme 4 · Issues, economy &amp; elections
title: Where Arizona stands on the issues

Directional 0–100 indices, each labeled with its direction. <b>Abortion</b> and <b>immigration</b> read higher = more restrictive; <b>water</b> (an Arizona-specific battery) and <b>gun control</b> read higher = more support. Also here: concern about violent crime, retrospective economic evaluations, confidence your vote is counted, trust in media, and <b>support for subverting election outcomes</b> (refusing to concede, legislators refusing to certify, violent protest). Water / gun / crime / personal-economy items are 2023-wave.

## feelings

eyebrow: Theme 5 · Feelings toward people &amp; groups
title: How warmly Arizonans feel — toward politicians, factions, and parties

Feeling thermometers, 0 (cold) to 100 (warm), by party. The factions add texture: Republicans run warmer toward <b>MAGA Republicans</b> than toward <b>Establishment Republicans</b>.

## metacog

eyebrow: Theme 6 · Metacognition
title: What partisans <em>think</em> the other side feels

Beliefs about the out-party, not feelings toward it. <b>Perceived partisan antipathy</b> is how much hostility respondents believe exists between the parties; <b>perceived out-party warmth</b> is how warm they think the other side feels toward their own. Below, the perception gap pits that guess against the out-party&rsquo;s <em>actual</em> mean warmth.

footerTemplate: Source: Arizona Voter Survey (The Arizona Voter Project, in collaboration with the Arizona Policy Lab at the University of Arizona; YouGov, 2023–2024).

The Arizona Voter Survey was fielded by <b>YouGov</b> over two waves (2023, 2024) of Arizona adults, matched and weighted to the state population; a subset of respondents recur across waves (a short <b>panel</b>), which is what lets us look at change. Every figure here is a group mean or share, small cells (&lt;{minCell}) suppressed — no individual responses are shown. The measures span partisan affect, democratic norms &amp; tolerance, issue positions, feeling thermometers, and metacognition.
